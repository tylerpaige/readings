Chapter 1: All Media is Training Data

All Media is Training Data

All media is training data. What kind of artwork could we make that acknowledges this? What do you make once you know that whatever you produce will be training machines and contributing to a model going forward?

Edward Snowden's 2013 NSA disclosures got us thinking seriously about data use and our own practice; the revelation that all of one's online activity could be accessed to inform, or entertain, an unknown third party. Anything we make that's machine legible, whether text, image, or sound, would now function not only as original writing, art, or music for a known audience, but also as training data for untold computational systems.

When we started building our own models around 2016, it was immediately clear that training data was going to become a site of legal and cultural tension. When you train a model, the first decision one has to make is this: what data do you use? Intuitively, it felt wrong to scrape our friends' work, but we also knew that having good data mattered, and so we attempted to produce our own for training purposes explicitly.

We started by putting together an ensemble of singers from different Berlin scenes. We were very clear that the purpose of the ensemble was to create training data. We wanted to demonstrate that one could produce rich and dynamic models when the data training was deliberate and consent was given up front. It felt appropriate to start a choir when we started training models, as, while it might be possible to automate the sound of group singing, participation was the point.

To our minds the training of models had the potential to be a new medium of performance and production.

Once you develop an understanding of how models are trained, and how training data is typically sourced, all kinds of questions emerge. For example, the emphasis on the truth of AI outputs raises questions about the truth of AI inputs, and how often the truth of collective creations is compressed into market-digestible fictions of individual accomplishment. Without intervention, we could end up with a future where the history on the AI substrate will have been written by the publicists!

Machines can only work from the information they are fed. We therefore have a responsibility going forward to be explicit and comprehensive about how things are created, and what we actually think about them. If we don't do that, models of the future will mirror our misrepresentations and elisions. This is a hopeful realisation. Far from the common fear that journalism, or writing, will be unnecessary in an AI-fueled future, we feel it is quite the opposite. The production of accurate and rich context will be as important as ever. AI models can ingest and summarise vast amounts of information. So it feels like an opportunity to be truthful and transparent in documentation of the complicated, rich, and collaborative ways that cultural projects come together. Journalism has a code of ethics, and so it is interesting to contemplate how we might all adopt codes of ethics considering that our contributions will be feeding future intelligences.

Artists are often assigned the role of dreamer, with little expectation of testing their ideals in non-art contexts. We've found that for our practice, however, it's only when we attempt to release systems into the world that we begin to understand on a new level the complexity of issues we care about. Building and deploying protocols for people to use is a way of making critique executable.

Starting Spawning.ai, and working (and clashing) directly with various tech companies, has required us to gain a more comprehensive knowledge of all the players in the AI field and the various motivations driving them. It is a fascinating challenge to conceive of something that might actually work, and a great creative risk to be vulnerable to the possibility that it will not.

The Call (2024)

1.2 Written c. 1570 by the English composer Thomas Tallis, 'Spem In Allum' is a 40-part motel, which is to say a multi-channel surround sound experience created well before the invention of speaker systems by coordinating forty human voices.

***

In 2023-2024 Herndon and Dryhurst collaborated with the Serpentine Arts Technologies team and curator Eva Jäger to produce The Call, presented at Serpentine in London in October 2024. The project, over a year in the making, aimed to shape new rituals for building artificial intelligence. It also proposed new cultural, legal, and technical practices for art generally, and for music specifically, in the age of AI.

For Herndon and Dryhurst, AI is a coordination and communication technology, much like singing has been for millennia. Across time, singing and its associated techniques, such as call and response, have served as rituals for communication, and in turn, humans have built infrastructure for singing to happen — spaces for gathering, processing, and transmitting information, as well as for creating meaning in social and civic life. As with a choir, where many individual voices form a collective, the artists propose that AI can further augment the transformation from the individual to the collective. The Call centred on developing new methods and materials for the creation of choral AI models. To train the models, Herndon and Dryhurst composed a songbook of hymns and singing exercises and worked with Arts Technologies Producer, Ruth Waters, and a sound recordist to develop a recording protocol, travelling to fifteen ensembles across the UK to capture their voices for a choral dataset. Serpentine's Future Art Ecosystems initiative provided the necessary legal and stewardship infrastructure to allow for contributing choristers to be part of an experimental Data Trust that works to distribute power between those included in the dataset and those who use the dataset and/or the models built from it.

The choral dataset was central to the models used to create an all-encompassing sound work that anchored Herndon and Dryhurst's exhibition at Serpentine North. Playing into the venue's chapel-like setting, the artists presented the many layers of human and machinic materials as artefacts for the new rituals necessary to train AI in a beautiful way. Designed by the architecture studio sub, a chandelier-like object in one room represented the recording protocol and allowed visitors to listen to spatialised audio from the dataset. In another room, visitors could demo one of the models through an altar-like interface, hearing their own inputs returned as choral harmonies. In the main exterior loop of the gallery, a new instrument — part organ, part GPU fan — stood for neural network compression and computation. Taken together, these elements made up the AI training trifecta: data > model > compute.

The idea for The Call — writing the songbook, designing the rig, recording live ensembles to create a bespoke dataset that we used to train a custom model, and then developing an installation strategy that enables anyone to engage with the work — is rooted in a desire to make the full process of generative AI legible. We're trying to be true to the beautiful human process of generative AI legible. To make very technical, ceremonial, abstract conversations into something visceral and emotional. For example, how can we make the steps to train generative AI visible and deample, how can we make the steps to train generative AI with themselves? What liberate to such an extent that they become an art form in themselves? What kind of schema or objects can we produce to ritualise these protocols? Obviously, the songbook is one. It draws from the Sacred Harp tradition of Obviously, the songbook is one. It draws from the Sacred Harp tradition of choral signing. And while we didn't use its convention of shape-note notation, it nevertheless inspired several other aspects of The Call: for one, we wrote our songs in a way that, like Sacred Harp, allows them to be collectively performed by people with a wide range of musical skills and literacies. It was important to us that both choristers who read and who do not read music could participate despite the music having sometimes a high degree of complexity. We also followed the Sacred Harp practice of organising performances so that the choristers face each other rather than an audience. We had choristers stand in two concentric circles. Not only did this work well for recording purposes, it increased communication and enabled the outer choir to provide a drone sound in the manner of certain Sacred Harp lineages.

Using the rig, we're able to record ensembles in two ways: there are individual microphones for each of the eight singers capturing the fullness and particular timbre of each voice; and a central ambisonic mic that records the ensemble at large, the unique ways each group tunes and syncs through performance. By standardising the sound input, the rig helps make sure that the training data are relatively consistent.

The great field recordist Alan Lomax was an important reference for us in creating this piece. Documenting the folk and blues singing of the mid-twentieth-century, he travelled the American South with a famous recording rig carried around in a suitcase. This was a very deliberate decision as it allowed him to record on the road, and he would plug the set-up into his car for power. Had he invited singers into a studio instead, they would have performed differently. Moreover, there wouldn't have been the sounds of chickens and other noise in the background that effectively became part of the music.

This made us think about how, in making a model, it's so important to capture information in as many dimensions as possible. If you were training a model to recognise a physical object, you'd want to photograph that object from every angle. Our subject being the human voice, we knew we needed to record both the individual singers and the collective output of each ensemble's performance. We also very purposefully wrote the songbook in a way that would enable choristers to use the full ranges of their voices and all of the phonemes in the English language. Human decision making is encouraged when The Call's choristers perform the songs; they are free to augment or subvert the score just as generations of musicians have adapted canonical hymns to their contemporary tastes for millenia.

1.6 Future Art Ecosystems: Art x Public AI (2024), is a Serpentine brief written by Victoria Ivanova, Eva Jäger, Alasdair Milne, and Gary Zhexi Zhang that puts forward strategies for artists and institutions to steward and prototype public-interest AI infrastructure, such as collectivised and democratic data governance.

1.7 'Bottom-Up Data Trusts: Disturbing the "One Size Fits All" Approach to Data Governance,' (International Data Privacy Law, Volume 9, Issue 4, 2019) by Sylvia Delacroix and Neil Lawrence, proposes using the legal framework of trusts to establish collective data rights and greater bargaining power for individuals.

We wanted to bring this same legibility to the elements in the exhibition, so we developed a representation of the recording rig together with sub's Niklas Bildstein Zaar. Its form resembles a chandelier, or bird feeder: it shows you where to stand and how to position your body in order to best feed the model. In the show, people will hear spatial audio of the field recordings. Standing in the middle of a Sacred Harp performance is incredibly moving. You're at the focal point of these information streams, awash in human energy and sound. We wanted to give people that visceral experience, and to physically connect the fieldwork that produced the training data for this model with the sounds that they are hearing the model generate.

The other aspect of the work is the governance of the choral recordings we collected. The Serpentine's Arts Tech team did research into data coalitions, and so we decided to undertake a Data Trust experiment. Essentially, the people who take the leap to contribute data — and, that's why the word 'trust' is so wonderful here — they realise they're producing something of value. They're aware that they are contributing to a shared dataset comprising the performances of all the different choirs we recorded throughout the United Kingdom, and that they will participate in the governance of that dataset together with the other choristers. They're aware that it's not only their individual voices that are valuable, but also their choirs' sound as a whole. There's a kind of unavoidable collectivity to dataset creation — a 'better together' scenario. In a data context, one person alone has little bargaining power, but 100,000 actually have a lot. That's a massive opportunity to network IP, and to think about how a data economy will work going forward.

One of the innovations we're testing in parallel with creating The Call is establishing a 'data trustee' for the work. This person serves as an assigned intermediary, and is delegated the duty of voting and making decisions on behalf of the group. The team hired Jennifer Ding to do this work with us, but since it's such a new role, she's defining the role together with the choristers. We're piloting a data trustee role with the Serpentine because all of this infrastructure first needs to be built and tested so that everyday people can manage their data in a way that's fair, yet not burdensome. We anticipate this kind of profession becoming more common, if not standard, looking forward.

Spawn | Spawn Training Ceremony I: Deep Belief (2018)

In 2016, Herndon and Dryhurst began training their own AI voice model, a singing neural network they named Spawn. They conceived of the endeavour as raising an 'AI baby' and worked with the machine learning developer Jules LaPlace to train Spawn on their friends' voices with the aim of having her join their human vocal ensemble as an inhuman collaborator. There was a great deal of care put into how Spawn would come to 'know' the world, and what information would serve as the basis of her artificial consciousness. In 2018-19, Herndon and Dryhurst staged a series of 'training ceremonies' — initially for the performance and installation Deep Belief at Gropius Bau in Berlin, then later for the PROTO global tour. During each session ensemble members led attendees in a series of call and response exercises (e.g., singing, emoting, reciting texts) that were recorded as original vocal samples and added to Spawn's dataset. So far, more than 50,000 voices have contributed to Spawn, some of which we can hear on Herndon's 2019 album PROTO (4AD) where Spawn has a central presence.

When engineers talk about training AI models, their concerns tend to be utilitarian and bureaucratic, like, 'how do we get enough data for this model to learn a voice'. The nature of that data is usually less important than its easy availability and legality of use. In training Spawn for PROTO, we saw the training itself as a performance, with everyone involved aware of their contribution. We felt we should even celebrate this action and ritualise it. Through the Deep Belief performances at Gropius Bau, we saw that when you do this openly and ask for consent, many people are happy to contribute to these processes. What we need now are prototypes for these new kinds of consenting interactions at scale.

HaveIBeenTrained.com | Spawning.ai (2022)

AI models today are trained on the vast amounts of data that are publicly available on the internet. It is quite likely that using these data is legal, largely due to AI development moving faster than policy and law. However, people want to have a say over whether and how their data are used and they currently have few options for doing so.

Herndon and Dryhurst, together with Jordan Meyer and Patrick Hoepner, co-founded Spawning in response to this problem. Their aim: to build a much-needed data governance layer for training generative AI. The company is named after a neologism Herndon and Dryhurst coined to explain a process that's central to their practice: the 'spawning' of generative systems — in contrast to the one-to-one mechanical reproduction of 'sampling'. Spawning launched in 2022 with its Have I Been Trained? tool, which allows users to search the most popular AI image dataset, LAION-5B, to determine whether their intellectual property is being used. Though data cannot be retrospectively removed from existing models (yet), a rights-holder can declare their preference for future training by adding selected results to Spawning's Do Not Train registry. These tools propose a basic protocol for data-use consent, making it simple for rights-holders to declare their preferences and for developers to omit opted-out material, should they choose, from new training rounds. Spawning's Do Not Train registry is honoured by both HuggingFace and Stability AI, and has been acknowledged in legal and policy circles as setting strong precedents towards consenting AI model training.

The discussion around AI data is often posed as 'copyright maximalists' stubbornly trying to maintain 20th century IP standards versus 'fair-use libertarians' eager to exploit fair-use laws to capitalist ends. But there is a more interesting possibility, and it involves inventing new IP standards native to this abstract new technology. Copyright, albeit a noble idea, is very old. To make a dad joke: the majority of piracy that was happening when copyright was invented was on literal ships in the sea. If the AI substrate will come to represent a new kind of internet, there will be many opportunities to explore IP in new ways. Binary conceptions of consent could evolve into far more nuanced permissions systems than the ones we currently know. We established Spawning to explore what is possible.

Kudurru (2023)

Since its launch in 2022, Spawning.ai has released several tools, which, together, propose a consent layer that is easy for both rights holders and developers to use.

Among these tools is the data defence system Kudurru.ai, which detects and intercepts bots scraping for data in its network. To achieve this, Spawning runs a network of more than a thousand sites hosting millions of media links found in the most popular datasets (such as LAION-5B) which are used to train generative AI models. Whenever a Kudurru site identifies a scraper, it collects the bot's IP address, blocklists it, and shares it with the rest of the network. Users can choose to simply block the blocklisted addresses, 'tarpit' them (slow them down), or 'poison' the dataset by supplying the scraper with false information. Since the network is decentralised, like a torrent or a blockchain, its function is uncompromised even as nodes come and go. The larger the network becomes, the greater its capacity to listen and intervene. In July 2023, Spawning tested the robustness of Kudurru with a performative attack. For about two hours, it successfully stopped everyone who was in the process of downloading the LAION-5B dataset, shutting down most AI image training globally and offering a glimpse of how a future web might function in the absence of shared data laws.

We viewed the Kudurru experiment as a performance because it happened in time and space — with the Spawning team and the model trainers as human participant-witnesses. But also, because it's a performative example of a web that we don't want, a web where people are constantly attacking each other, poisoning each other, shutting each other down. This is ultimately what we risk if we do not establish protocols of consent around data. Nobody with valuable data will accept this: instead, they will hide their data behind firewalls, and will attack anything that tries to obtain it. Our intention was to model this scenario. We hoped to demonstrate that coming to civil terms over data consent is in everyone's interest.

Kudurru is a trickster splinter project of Spawning. Many take the opinion that everything on the web is fair game for AI training. In some cases, they might be legally right, because the law has not caught up to technological capabilities. Yet if one proposal for data rights is that the open internet is fair game, then why not experiment with using the open internet creatively as a tool to counter-scraping?

There was a Filipino composer named José Maceda who created a piece called Ugnayan, for 20 Radio Stations. It utilised all of the public radio frequencies in metropolitan Manila for about an hour on New Year's Day 1974. Maceda was friends with Imelda Marcos, so, with her help, he basically made the whole country his multichannel audio piece. While this may have been a questionable thing to do at a time when radio was the public's primary listening source, it also had this incredible, beautiful effect only achieved by taking over the entire network. It is interesting to think about an artistic gesture operating across networks at that scale. Kudurru's listening and coordination capacities have an elegance to them.

The River and The Manifold: Notes on Generative AI
(Before it Really Exists)

– Benjamin Bratton

Always remember everything you do from the moment you wake up to the moment you fall asleep, is training data for the future's model of today.

Do you remember the dustup when Scarlett Johannson's voice was used for GPT-4o because Sam Altman thought it would be cool to have OpenAI's model talk like Samantha from Her? As it turned out, things were not as they seemed at first blush.

Apparently it was never actually Johannson's voice, but another voice actor; and it was recorded before Altman asked Johannson to use her voice; and it's been out for months as part of GPT-4o. And so....the story was wrong.

If, as many AI critics have told us, the first version of the tale proved that We Are All Scarlet, the platforms are stealing our very beings to sell back to us, and we must reclaim our stolen dignity from the math monsters, what does this new version prove?

Perhaps it proves that human culture is actually not made up of individually owned bits of property and speech acts but is, and always has been, a manifold, a river, an uneven fabric from which individuality briefly emerges.

LLMs model the manifold, not the sign or the person doing the signifying. This is deeply confounding for many smart people, and for good reason. It will require a serious rethink of what the fuzzy continuum of human cultural intelligence really is. The implications are particularly profound for generative AI, which is both trained on and productive of whatever 'culture' is.

How did we get to this particular fork in the road? Let's take a step back and survey the land.

How Many AIs?

The Stack, the architecture of planetary computation, is being replaced layer-by-layer by a new system: an AI Stack. Neuromorphic chips, giant models in data centres, very big models running locally on your mobile, prompting replacing programming, and eventually, and with real time learning, perhaps also augmenting foundational training runs.

One possible scenario is that the Compute necessary to train and serve GAI-adjacent models is so great that only state scale actors such as Google, Baidu, or OpenAI can do it. How many VLM (Very Large Models) does the planet need to run everything it needs? 5? 10? 100?

There was a Simpsons episode in which they made a joke about futurist predictions. One of the characters, I can't remember which, said something along the lines of 'why would the world ever need more than 10 computers? One for each of the Kings of Europe?' If you cross out the Europe part, they may have gotten it right after all.

Another scenario is that there will be more like 100 billion little models, 10 or so per planetary capita. For this massively intricate ecology of tiny minds, interacting with us and with each other, unforeseeable forms of language, alliances and collaborations may emerge.

Both scenarios may be realised, and more, and also less. For either, the present relationship between the three primary modes of user-AI interaction — prompting, training, and searching — would fundamentally change. They will converge, diverge, and evolve in ways not well-addressed by the conventional debates about generative AI.

The distances between training data and prompt engineering can seem, at different times, to be vast or narrow. To train or to prompt are both forms of interaction with large models. As the collective intelligence of culture is transformed into weights, weights are activated by prompts shared across domains to produce new artefacts. Though the interface culture of private prompting tends to individuate interactions with models, there are many ways to design massively-distributed prompting, producing collective artefacts that mirror the societal-scale intelligence of training data.

All this may change quickly once something like real-time learning is solved, whereby model weights are changed by the very act of using the model. This implies not one giant model to rule them all, but a federation of many tiny models, each diverging on its own and feeding back into the whole in limited ways.

An Atlas of How Theorists of A Certain Age Believe Computers Work

I have told this story before, but it's worth retelling in this context. Back when I was teaching at the European Graduate School, my time overlapped with Slavoj Žižek with whom I had many memorable conversations (for me at least). One night he gave a talk on 'Google Search and Psychoanalysis', or some variation on that. About fifteen minutes in, it was clear that he had no idea how Google Search works, but had constructed in his mind an incredible, intricate Borgesian machine, one that existed solely due to the force of his imagination. He then gave us a long and lucid critique of a technology that is not real, but seemed real to him. Over the years I have encountered so many similar examples of elaborate critiques of imaginary technologies that the pattern seems to me more than coincidental.

I recently had a strange conversation with a very well known artist who had written a piece about their experience with general purpose models that left me confused, but perhaps not as confused as them. They learned that some of their work was used as training data for GPT4. They then prompted GPT4 to create images in their style. The results were pretty crap.

The artist concluded that this is due to the fact that the models and the model-builders just don't understand that real art is not just about statistical regularity. The nerds' tunnel vision threatens to flatten culture. The artist then asked the model for a portrait of themselves. They were even more horrified by these results, and levelled up their criticism, outraged that identity as a whole could be mangled in this way.

Some of my conversations with them involved the discovery that they misunderstood how models and modelling works, and so, like Žižek, had convinced themselves of some dark conclusions which they aired in public. In short, they did not understand the difference between training of models and model weights. They believed that somewhere in the model, the AI Company was storing complete, stolen copies of their work, including many non-consensually captured images of the artist themself. To prompt the model was analogous to Google Image Search. The User asks for 'an image in the style of X artist', and the big machine looks up the samples it is keeping hostage, and then, because it can only think through dumb nerd math, cooks up an insultingly poor rendition. The same goes for the artist's portrait. This is not how it works. If this were how it works, then their alarmed response would be wholly justified. In fact, however, their alarm is symptomatic of what we may now call a Žižekian fantasy technology.

Culture as Manifold

It is important to emphasise that one of the most important things that LLMs clarify is that 'culture' is not the sum of individual pieces of private property known as 'works', but is spread across the the intricate accumulations of biosemiotic intelligence, including, but by no means limited to, all the things we call literature, language, and art. Culture is a manifold. Back in the day, Deconstruction made an analogous point, one that is forgotten by Derrideans now disoriented by ChatGPT.

I explained to my artist friend that their works are not being held inside the model, but rather that they have been abstracted into mathematical patterns of regularity, specificity, variation, and generic form, and that these abstractions — not the works themselves — are added to the massive matrix of weights that constitutes this still low-dimensional approximation of culture. Ultimately, in this context, the abstraction of their work is the math that describes their works' similarity and difference from all other work. It is not treated any differently than the other samples thrown into the big mix. This only made them more angry, and more sad; perhaps not because of what AI was doing to their art, but because of what AI revealed their work to be: just more semiotic stuff.

That is, the reason LLM is able to produce outputs that look like culture when it treats culture as a manifold rather than as an encyclopedia is because culture really is a manifold more than it is an encyclopedia.

I went on to explain that the 'AI art' that GPT4 makes is not only trained on 'art'. People are making 'art' based on training data drawn from all kinds of sources, just as people are making other things based on data derived from training on 'art'. Art is not special, other than as a kind of 'beginner's format' for playing with large models and making something that feels creative (and probably is), such as your parents' first Midjourney doodles.

What, then, are people doing when they do this? If the whole AI-Art cycle departs so decisively from the Modern norm of individual vitalist inspiration, what kind of 'creativity' is this? It would seem that it generalises inspiration to 'formatted intentionality', something that would have to be part of any definition of both 'prompt' and 'search'.

Intentionality and Intertextuality

Boris Groys makes a similar point when he observes that prompting means the overt translation of user intentionality into an output as an image or text (or other modality). He suggests that this instrumentalisation of intentionality subverts the supposed 'death of the author', for which any text only exists in intertextual relationship with others, and cannot be properly understood through the 'original intention' of a singular signatory author. Clearly, Groys notes, 'intention' is the decisive trick of producing any outputs with GPT.

Following his lead, the focus on intentionality also questions the widespread preliminary diagnosis that generative AI does away with 'the myth of the author', because people are creating texts with automated machines and not the spark of unique intuition. When the final copy is a mixture of machine- and human-made sentences, the vitalist idea that creativity springs from one person's inner voice loses its footing. But you see the problem here. Is generative AI really, then, about a return of 'intentionality' in the instrumentalisation of wish via the prompt, or is it a radicalization of intertextuality through the revelation of culture as manifold? The answer is 'yes' it's both.

One path to resolving this dilemma is to recognise that a user is not the same thing as an author, and so one does not cleanly superimpose onto the other. What is confusing is that generative AI situates the user in places where we are used to seeing the author, and perhaps vice versa, and so comparisons are inevitable. This distinction and dynamic deserves more careful development than I can fold into my word count limit, but it is hard to overstress that AI is not only the medium used by a user/author, and it is not only the repository of intertextual potentialities that they activate. AI can also be the User. AI itself uses generative AI to make things. AI conducts searches. AI finds things. AI programs. AI debugs. 'The Model is the Message', as Blaise Aguera y Arcas and I wrote, but sometimes also the model is the messenger.

Artist as Model As Art

Now artists are training models of themselves and their work and making these available to other users to produce unforeseen new works. In doing so, should we anticipate artistic and cultural practices, as a whole, evolving into intentionally produced models? Holly Herndon and Mat Dryhurt's practice is a forerunner in this rotation, and in time, this reconstruction of an artistic practice and the persona of the practitioner via generative AI may end up being one of their signal creative contributions.

I am not about to switch into Art Critic Mode, and so I will not index everything they have done in this regard. I will presume the reader knows or can find out about it if they want to. A longer treatment might compare the construction of artist-as-model to The Factory system devised by Warhol, from which any number of works could be generated when prompted by different collaborators.

The figure of the Artist as a truth-telling avatar of sovereign intentionality is the real message of any sufficiently capitalised biennale. This is why the topic of evacuating the figure of the Artist on behalf of the production of 'art' remains a third rail. It is ontologically and economically dangerous. What is at stake is not only the livelihoods of people in creative industries, but upstream of them, also at stake is the elevated status of the Artist and thus the rarefication of the gestures they produce. What is at stake is ultimately the institution of art itself, and so no wonder AI is targeted as the dark symbol of monopower by art world reactionaries.

'Artist as model as art' poses rather different relationships between cultural producer, cultural production, and cultural consumption. Some possibilities may overwhelm legacy art world economies, while others will never make it past the green shoots phase, because big art fairs can't sell them and influential art journals can't manufacture romantic critiques of the modern world from them. At the same time, the transformations brought by the interactive automation of multimodal mixing of image, sound, text, and video may be really, really boring yet really, really popular.

What they may accomplish, for better and for worse, is to hyper-individuate the cybernetic cycle of cultural desire and provision to such a degree that just as streaming music traded the album for the playlist, the coming years may trade the Artist for the recommendation stream itself. The music that kids born this year may prefer will not be made up of discrete songs by discrete artists, but is rather an endless flow of genre types, acoustic textures, instrumental and vocal layers, all morphing into one another in an endless river of sound made just for you. I don't for a moment suppose that is what Herndon and Dryhurst have in mind, but these things are seldom really under anyone's control. Generative AI music may be more like a river than a jukebox, into which you step your toe and submerge your ear when you like, and when doing so, you change the course of the river through the reinforcement of your changing affective preferences. By the time today's children reach premature retirement, they may each be listening, with great and appreciative attention, to a sonic bath so finely tuned for their nervous systems that no one else alive, not even their closest friends, could stand to hear it for more than a few minutes.
